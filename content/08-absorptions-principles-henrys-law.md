## Pretest
**Q:** What property does absorption exploit, compared with distillation?
**A:** The difference in solubility of the components in a liquid. Distillation exploits volatility. [Week 3, p. 2; Week 1, p. 44]

**Q:** You raise the absorber pressure. Does more or less gas dissolve?
**A:** More. $K = H_A/P_{tot}$ falls, so the equilibrium liquid concentration for a given $y$ rises. [practice exam 2 solutions.pdf, Q3e.1]

**Q:** A dissolved gas has a negative enthalpy of solution. Heating the absorber does what to its solubility?
**A:** Lowers it. The Henry constant increases with temperature. [Week 3, p. 23; exercise_03_solutions.pdf, Oppg. 1c]

## Mental model
In absorption a gas mixture contacts a liquid solvent (absorbent), and the components that dissolve best (solutes, absorbates) transfer from the gas to the liquid [Week 3, p. 2]. Like distillation, it needs equilibrium data, but here they describe gas solubility [Week 3, p. 7]. Gases usually dissolve only a little, so the liquid is dilute and the equilibrium is a straight line, Henry's law: partial pressure ∝ liquid mole fraction, with a large coefficient $H_A$ for poorly soluble gases [Week 3, p. 7]. Dividing by total pressure gives the distribution coefficient $K_A = H_A/P_{tot}$. A small $K_A$ means a soluble gas and easy absorption [FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 2, eq. 16]. High pressure and low temperature both lower $K_A$, which is why absorbers run cold and pressurised and strippers run hot or at low pressure [practice exam 2 solutions.pdf, Q3e; Week 3, p. 6, 23]. If the solvent reacts reversibly with the solute, it can be regenerated by stripping (desorption). If the reaction is irreversible, it can't [Week 3, p. 5–6].

## Concepts and definitions
- **Absorption**: separation based on differences in solubility in the liquid phase. Gas and liquid (solvent or absorbent) are in direct contact, and mass transfer goes from gas to liquid. The transferred components are solutes or absorbates. Batch or continuous, the latter in a column similar to distillation. [Week 3, p. 2]
- **Main application**: gas cleaning, e.g. removal of SO₂, H₂S, CO₂. Gas enters at the bottom and leaves at the top. Liquid enters at the top and leaves at the bottom carrying the captured components. [Week 3, p. 3]
- **Column types**: packed column (structured or unstructured packing creates the gas–liquid interface), plate column (plates as in distillation), spray column (dusjtårn, absorbent sprayed at the top). The internal structure determines mass transfer rates. [Week 3, p. 3–4]
- **Physical absorption**: the absorbed components are more soluble than the others but don't react with the solvent. [Week 3, p. 5]
- **Reversible reaction**: reversible chemical reaction between the absorbed gas and a reactant in the solvent. [Week 3, p. 5]
- **Irreversible reaction**: irreversible reaction between absorbate and a reactant in the solvent, e.g. nitric acid production by absorbing NO₂ in water. [Week 3, p. 5]
- **Stripping (desorption)**: removing absorbates from the solvent again. Only possible for physical and reversible absorption. Achieved by lowering the (partial) pressure, heating to generate a stripping vapour, or stripping with an inert gas. [Week 3, p. 6]
- **Henry's law**: equilibrium for gases of low solubility, with $H_A$ the Henry coefficient. It can be written in several forms (pressure or concentration), so always check which version is used. It ignores reactions in the liquid, so actual absorption can be higher than predicted. [Week 3, p. 7–8]
- **Distribution coefficient $K_A$**: $K_A = H_A/P_{tot}$, giving $y_A = K_A x_A$. [FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 2, eq. 16; Week 3, p. 23]
- **Enthalpy of solution (løsningsentalpien, $\Delta H$)**: for most gases $\Delta H < 0$, so $K_A$ increases with temperature and solubility drops. [Week 3, p. 23]

## Equations

### Raoult's law (compare)
$$y_A = \frac{P_A^\circ}{P_{tot}}\, x_A$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $P_A^\circ$ | vapour pressure of pure A | atm or bar |
| $P_{tot}$ | total pressure | atm or bar |

The distillation-style equilibrium, shown for comparison. [Week 3, p. 7]

### Henry's law (pressure form)
$$p_A = H_A x_A \qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad y_A = \frac{H_A}{P_{tot}}\, x_A$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $p_A$ | partial pressure of A in the gas | atm |
| $H_A$ | Henry coefficient (pressure/mole-fraction form) | atm |
| $x_A$ | mole fraction of A in the liquid | – |
| $y_A$ | mole fraction of A in the gas | – |

Low solubility (dilute liquid), no reaction in the liquid. Values at 298.15 K are in Table 2 of the formula sheet. [Week 3, p. 7–8; FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 2, eq. 15, and p. 11, Table 2]

### Equilibrium with distribution coefficient
$$y_A = K_A x_A, \qquad K_A = \frac{H_A}{P_{tot}}$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $K_A$ | vapour–liquid distribution coefficient | – |

[FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 2, eq. 16; Week 3, p. 23]

### Van 't Hoff (temperature dependence of Henry/K)
$$\frac{d\ln K_A}{dT} = -\frac{\Delta H}{RT^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad K_A(T_2) = K_A(T_1)\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{\Delta H}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right)\right]$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $K_A(T)$ | distribution coefficient (or $H_A$) at temperature T | – (or atm) |
| $\Delta H$ | enthalpy of solution | J/mol |
| $R$ | gas constant, 8.314 | J/(mol·K) |
| $T_1, T_2$ | temperatures | K |

For most gases $\Delta H < 0$, so K increases with T. The same form is used for $H_A$. [Week 3, p. 23; exercise_03_solutions.pdf, Oppg. 1c, eq. 4]

## Worked example
**Mole fraction of oxygen in water in equilibrium with air** [Week 3, p. 9–10]
Water at 25 °C, air at 1 atm.
1. Air is 21 mol % O₂.
2. Partial pressure (Dalton): $p_{O_2} = y_{O_2} P_{tot} = 0.21 \cdot 1 = 0.21$ atm.
3. Read the solubility figure at 25 °C: $1/H \approx 2.0\cdot 10^{-5}$ atm⁻¹, so $H_{O_2} \approx 50\,000$ atm.
4. Henry: $p_{O_2} = H_{O_2} x_{O_2}$, so $0.21 = 50\,000 \cdot x_{O_2}$.
5. $x_{O_2} = 4.2 \cdot 10^{-6}$.
6. Sense check: a large H means a poorly soluble gas and a tiny $x$. The formula sheet lists $H_{O_2} = 4.259\cdot 10^4$ atm at 298.15 K, the same order of magnitude. [FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 11, Table 2]

## Faded example
**CO₂ absorber: Henry coefficient, K and process changes** [practice exam 2 solutions.pdf, Q3a, Q3e.1–2]
Absorber at 8 °C and 15 atm, pure water solvent, gas 12 mol % CO₂.
1. From the solubility figure near 10 °C: $1/H_{CO_2} = 10^{-3}$ atm⁻¹.
2. **(try)** Henry coefficient. → $H_{CO_2} = 1000$ atm.
3. **(try)** Distribution coefficient. → $K = H/P_{tot} = 1000/15 = 66.7$.
4. **(try)** The pressure rises to 20 atm. More or less CO₂ removed? → More. $K = 1000/20 = 50$ is lower, so a higher equilibrium liquid concentration is possible at the same $y$.
5. **(try)** The temperature rises to 15 °C. More or less? → Less. A higher temperature gives a higher Henry constant and a higher K.

## Exam traps
- **The figure shows $1/H$, not H**: read the reciprocal and invert it. $10^{-3}$ atm⁻¹ means $H = 1000$ atm. [practice exam 2 solutions.pdf, Q3a; Week 3, p. 10]
- **Use the partial pressure**: Henry relates $p_A$ (e.g. $0.21$ atm O₂), not $P_{tot}$, to $x_A$. [Week 3, p. 10]
- **Check the Henry form**: pressure vs concentration forms have different units and values. The formula sheet table is the pressure/mole-fraction form in atm at 298.15 K. [Week 3, p. 8; FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 11, Table 2]
- **K depends on pressure, H doesn't**: $K = H/P_{tot}$. Higher P gives lower K and more absorption. [practice exam 2 solutions.pdf, Q3e.1]
- **Temperature direction**: $\Delta H < 0$ for most gases, so higher T gives higher H and K and lower solubility. Don't flip the sign in the exponential. [Week 3, p. 23; exercise_03_solutions.pdf, Oppg. 1c]
- **The formula sheet's van 't Hoff is a different one**: eq. 78 is the crystallization solubility version ($\ln x_s$ with $\Delta H_{melt}$). The Henry/K temperature equation is not on the sheet. [FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 8, eq. 78; Week 3, p. 23]
- **Reactions break Henry**: chemical absorption can take up more than Henry predicts. [Week 3, p. 8]
- **Irreversible means not regenerable**: stripping only works for physical and reversible absorption. [Week 3, p. 5–6]

## Test yourself
**Q:** A soluble gas A with $p_A = 0.15$ atm is in equilibrium with water at 298.15 K. Find $x_A$ for SO₂ ($H = 45.01$ atm) and NH₃ ($H = 0.893$ atm).
**A:** SO₂: $x_A = 0.15/45.01 = 0.0033$. NH₃: $x_A = 0.15/0.893 = 0.17$. [exercise_03_solutions.pdf, Oppg. 1a]

**Q:** Convert $x_{SO_2} = 0.0033$ in water to mass %.
**A:** $m\% = 100\cdot \dfrac{0.0033\cdot 64.1}{0.0033\cdot 64.1 + 0.9967\cdot 18} = 1.16\,\%$. [exercise_03_solutions.pdf, Oppg. 1b]

**Q:** Name the three interaction types and say which can be regenerated.
**A:** Physical absorption, reversible reaction and irreversible reaction. The first two can be stripped. [Week 3, p. 5–6]

**Q:** Name three ways to strip an absorbate from a solvent.
**A:** Lower the (partial) pressure, heat to generate a stripping vapour, or strip with an inert gas. [Week 3, p. 6]

**Q:** Why might measured CO₂ uptake in an amine solvent exceed what Henry's law predicts?
**A:** Henry's law doesn't account for reactions in the liquid phase, so chemical absorption can take up more. [Week 3, p. 8]
