## Pretest
**Q:** Is adsorption exothermic or endothermic? What does that mean for regeneration?
**A:** Exothermic (releases heat). Desorption is endothermic and needs heat, so heating is one way to regenerate. [Week 7, p. 3, 5]

**Q:** Which binds more strongly and more selectively: physisorption or chemisorption?
**A:** Chemisorption (ionic or covalent bonds). [Week 7, p. 4]

**Q:** Typical specific surface area of an adsorbent?
**A:** About 300–1200 m²/g. [Week 7, p. 6]

## Mental model
In absorption the solute dissolves *into* a liquid. In adsorption it sticks *onto* the surface of an insoluble solid [Week 7, p. 2–3]. Molecules land and leave all the time. The balance between the two rates is a dynamic equilibrium, much like evaporation and condensation in VLE [Week 7, p. 3]. Since everything happens on the surface, an adsorbent is all about area. Pores give a gram of solid hundreds of m² of wall, and the smaller the pores, the more area per gram [Week 7, p. 6, 9–10]. Two numbers describe a good adsorbent: capacity (how much fits per kg, i.e. binding sites) and selectivity (how much more it likes the target than the rest, a ratio of equilibrium constants) [Week 7, p. 6]. A saturated bed is useless until regenerated, so the binding must be reversible. You shift the equilibrium by changing temperature or pressure/concentration, and you run several beds to stay continuous [Week 7, p. 5].

## Concepts and definitions
- **Adsorption**: mass transfer of a component (adsorbate) onto insoluble solid particles (adsorbent). [Week 7, p. 3]
- **Desorption**: transfer of components away from the particle surface. [Week 7, p. 3]
- **Heat effects**: adsorption is exothermic and releases heat. Desorption is endothermic and requires heat. [Week 7, p. 3]
- **Dynamic equilibrium**: adsorption and desorption rates depend on process conditions, similar to VLE with constant evaporation and condensation. [Week 7, p. 3]
- **Physisorption**: Van der Waals or electrostatic forces, relatively weak, doesn't change the chemical identity. Example: hydrogen on activated carbon. [Week 7, p. 4]
- **Chemisorption**: ionic or covalent bonds, relatively strong forces and high selectivity, needs higher temperatures. Example: in hydrogenation of an alkene. [Week 7, p. 4]
- **Bed and regeneration**: particles packed in a column form a bed. When saturated, the bed is regenerated by changing temperature or pressure/concentration. Reversibility is important, and several beds are often needed for continuous operation. [Week 7, p. 5]
- **Selectivity**: preference of an adsorbent for a target molecule. For binary A+B, $\alpha_A = K_A/K_B$. [Week 7, p. 6; FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 6, eq. 63]
- **Capacity**: amount of adsorbed molecules possible per mass of adsorbent, i.e. number of binding sites per mass. More surface area means more binding sites. [Week 7, p. 6]
- **Volumes in a bed**: solid particle $V_s$, empty space in the particle $V_{pore}$, and empty space between particles $V_{void}$. [Week 7, p. 9]
- **Specific surface area S**: pore surface per mass of particle (m²/g), assuming cylindrical pores. [Week 7, p. 10; FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 6, eq. 64]
- **Common adsorbents**: (1) activated carbon, hydrophobic (pellets, spheres, cylinders, powder); (2) silica gels, mildly hydrophobic or hydrophilic, often for water removal from gases; (3) activated alumina, hydrophilic, microporous Al₂O₃; (4) molecular-sieve zeolites, hydrophilic, crystalline regular structures whose channel size may exclude bulky molecules, with strong bonds to polar molecules. [Week 7, p. 13]
- **Pore classes**: micropores < 2 nm (high surface area, limited transport volume); mesopores 2–50 nm; macropores > 50 nm (limited surface area, large transport volume). [Week 7, p. 14]
- **Water adsorption**: silica and alumina have high capacity and are easier to regenerate. Zeolites bind water strongly, giving high water removal. [Week 7, p. 15]

## Equations

### Selectivity
$$\alpha_A = \frac{K_A}{K_B}$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $\alpha_A$ | selectivity of the adsorbent toward A over B | – |
| $K_A, K_B$ | adsorption equilibrium (isotherm) constants | e.g. bar⁻¹ or m³/mol (same for both) |

Binary mixture. [Week 7, p. 6; FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 6, eq. 63]

### Cylindrical pore geometry
$$V_{pore} = \tfrac{1}{4}\pi d^2 L, \qquad S_{pore} = \pi d L, \qquad \frac{S_{pore}}{V_{pore}} = \frac{4}{d}$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $d$ | pore diameter | m |
| $L$ | pore length | m |

[Week 7, p. 10]

### Specific surface area
$$S = \frac{S_{pore}}{m_{part}} = \frac{4\,\epsilon_{part}}{d\,\rho_{part}}, \qquad V_{pore} = \epsilon_{part}V_{part}, \quad V_{part} = \frac{m_{part}}{\rho_{part}}$$
| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $S$ | specific surface area | m²/g |
| $\epsilon_{part}$ | particle porosity (pore volume / particle volume) | – |
| $\rho_{part}$ | particle density | g/m³ |
| $d$ | pore diameter | m |

Assumes cylindrical pores. [Week 7, p. 10; FormelarkKP3010.pdf, p. 6, eq. 64]

## Worked example
**Specific surface area of a porous solid** [Week 7, p. 11–12]
$\epsilon_{part} = 0.6$, $\rho_{part} = 1.2$ g/cm³, $d_{pore} = 4$ nm.
1. Convert density: $1.2$ g/cm³ $= 1.2\cdot 10^6$ g/m³.
2. Convert diameter: $4$ nm $= 4\cdot 10^{-9}$ m.
3. Insert: $S = \dfrac{4\cdot 0.6}{4\cdot 10^{-9}\cdot 1.2\cdot 10^6} = 500$ m²/g.
4. Check: within the typical 300–1200 m²/g, and 4 nm is a mesopore. [Week 7, p. 6, 14]

## Faded example
**Capacity and selectivity from two Langmuir isotherms (N₂/O₂)** [practice_exam_solutions.pdf, Ex. 5a]
$q_{N_2} = \dfrac{100\,p_{N_2}}{1 + 2p_{N_2}}$ and $q_{O_2} = \dfrac{250\,p_{O_2}}{1 + 10p_{O_2}}$, with q in mol/kg.
1. Compare with $q = \dfrac{q_{max}Kp}{1 + Kp}$.
2. **(try)** Equilibrium constants. → $K_{N_2} = 2$ and $K_{O_2} = 10$.
3. **(try)** Capacities. → $q_{max,N_2} = 100/2 = 50$ mol/kg and $q_{max,O_2} = 250/10 = 25$ mol/kg.
4. **(try)** Selectivity of N₂ over O₂. → $S = 2/10 = 0.2$.
5. **(try)** Interpret. → The adsorbent holds *more* N₂ at saturation (capacity) but *prefers* O₂ (selectivity < 1).

## Exam traps
- **Capacity ≠ selectivity**: capacity is $q_{max}$ (binding sites per kg). Selectivity uses only the K values. [Week 7, p. 6; practice_exam_solutions.pdf, Ex. 5a]
- **Units in S**: density must be in g/m³ (× 10⁶ from g/cm³) and d in m (× 10⁻⁹ from nm). Then S comes out in m²/g. [Week 7, p. 12]
- **d is the pore diameter, not the particle diameter**. [Week 7, p. 10–11]
- **Small pores give more area but slower transport**: micropores have high area and limited transport volume, macropores the opposite. [Week 7, p. 14]
- **Hydrophobic vs hydrophilic**: activated carbon is hydrophobic. Silica, alumina and zeolites are hydrophilic, so they are used for water removal. [Week 7, p. 13, 15]
- **Regeneration direction**: adsorption is exothermic, so raising T (or lowering p/c) desorbs. [Week 7, p. 3, 5]
- **Slide wording**: Week 7, p. 4 calls physisorption "intramolecular" and chemisorption "intermolecular". The physical content (weak VdW/electrostatic vs strong ionic/covalent bonds) is what matters. It also writes "absorption of hydrogen on activated carbon" where adsorption is meant. [Week 7, p. 4]

## Test yourself
**Q:** Give the selectivity toward A for $K_A = 1/6$, $K_B = 3/5$.
**A:** $\alpha_A = (1/6)/(3/5) = 0.28$. [Week 7, p. 8]

**Q:** Derive $S/V = 4/d$ for a cylindrical pore.
**A:** $S = \pi dL$ and $V = \frac{1}{4}\pi d^2L$, so $S/V = 4/d$. [Week 7, p. 10]

**Q:** Same material as the worked example but with 2 nm pores. What is S?
**A:** S doubles to 1000 m²/g, because $S \propto 1/d$. [Week 7, p. 10–12]

**Q:** Which adsorbent would you choose for deep drying of a gas, and which for easy regeneration?
**A:** Zeolites for strong water binding and high removal. Silica or alumina for high capacity and easier regeneration. [Week 7, p. 15]

**Q:** Name two ways to regenerate a saturated bed, and why you need several beds.
**A:** Change the temperature, or the pressure/concentration. Multiple beds keep the operation continuous while one regenerates. [Week 7, p. 5]
